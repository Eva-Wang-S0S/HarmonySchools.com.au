Case Study 2 – The Family Learning Club: Building Bridges Through Learning

Setting: Victoria | Focus: School–Community Partnership with Refugee Families

Explore this case study
Case Narrative

When Hawa, a Somali mother of three, first stepped into Preston North East Primary School in Melbourne’s northern suburbs, she gripped her daughter’s hand tightly. She smiled politely at the receptionist but hesitated when handed an English-only notice about parent–teacher interviews.

Her eight-year-old daughter Asha quietly translated a few words, but Hawa’s anxiety grew. “My English not good… maybe next time,” she murmured, retreating quickly.

Teachers at the school had noticed similar patterns among several families who had recently arrived from Somalia, Afghanistan, and Myanmar. Students were eager but struggled with homework, and their parents rarely attended school events. Teachers wanted to help but didn’t know how to reach families who felt uncertain or unwelcome in formal school settings.

That changed when the school joined the Refugee Education Support Program (RESP) in partnership with the Centre for Multicultural Youth (CMY) and the Spectrum Migrant Resource Centre. A Family Learning Club (FLC) was proposed — an after-school program where children and parents could learn together in a relaxed, bilingual environment.

The school social worker, Ms Tran, coordinated the pilot. She liaised with teachers, community organisations, and volunteer tutors. The sessions were scheduled on Thursday afternoons, with interpreters and bilingual volunteers present.

On the first day, only three families came. Hawa sat quietly at the back, watching Asha read English flashcards with a volunteer. After a few sessions, Ms Tran invited Hawa to join a simple literacy activity — tracing English letters alongside her daughter. The room filled with laughter when Hawa shyly attempted the word “elephant” and Asha clapped for her mother.

As weeks passed, attendance doubled. Parents began asking questions about school expectations and homework routines. Ms Tran facilitated short discussions — translated into Somali, Dari, and Arabic — about helping children learn at home, healthy routines, and settlement supports.

By the end of the term, Hawa was helping to welcome new families to the club. “Before, I was afraid to come to school,” she said during an evaluation session. “Now I feel like this is our place too.”

Professional Analysis

Presenting Problem & Context Factors:

  • Low parental engagement among refugee families due to language barriers, limited literacy, and unfamiliarity with the Australian school system.
  • Teachers lacked cross-cultural communication strategies, while families felt intimidated by formal education structures.
  • Limited trust between newly arrived parents and the school, compounded by disrupted or traumatic prior schooling experiences.

Social Work Assessment: Ecological Systems Model (Bronfenbrenner) and strengths-based, community development practice

  • Family settlement stress and social isolation.
  • Parents’ prior educational experiences and confidence.
  • Institutional accessibility (interpreters, signage, timing, transport).
  • Families’ protective strengths (commitment to children’s education, strong community networks, motivation to learn).

Intervention & Social Worker Roles:

Stage Social Worker Actions & Roles
Community engagement Consulted CMY and Spectrum MRC to co-design a culturally responsive after-school Family Learning Club; sought funding for interpreters, snacks, and childcare to reduce participation barriers.
Program coordination Facilitated weekly FLC sessions; organised volunteer tutors; ensured interpreters and bilingual aides were present; liaised with teachers to align activities with classroom learning.
Capacity building Delivered brief parent education sessions (translated into Somali, Dari, and Arabic) on homework routines, understanding school reports, and supporting learning at home.
Advocacy Advocated within the school for translated notices, use of interpreters for phone calls, and more welcoming entry spaces for families; encouraged flexible scheduling of interviews.
Evaluation Used participatory evaluation (story sharing, feedback circles, simple rating tools) to capture parent experiences; incorporated feedback into ongoing program design and school planning.

Outcomes:

  • Families reported increased confidence communicating with teachers and attending school events.
  • Students showed improved homework completion, engagement, and English language progress.
  • School staff reported stronger empathy and awareness of refugee settlement challenges.
  • The school embedded the Family Learning Club as a sustained school–community partnership model, rather than a one-off project.
Reflection Questions
  • How does shared learning help build trust and reduce power imbalance between schools and CALD families?
  • What ethical and logistical issues might arise when using volunteers and bilingual aides in school-based programs?
  • In what ways can social workers sustain parental engagement beyond the life of a funded program?
  • How could evaluation tools be adapted to capture outcomes that are meaningful to culturally diverse families?
